Commuting operators and simultaneous diagonalization

Working note · Edit and expand as your understanding develops

Two operators that commute can often be diagonalized in a common basis when the operators are suitably well behaved—for example, finite-dimensional Hermitian operators. This is one reason commutation matters in quantum mechanics.

The linear-algebra idea

Suppose two Hermitian operators \(A\) and \(B\) satisfy \(AB=BA\). Each eigenspace of \(A\) is invariant under \(B\): if \(A|v\rangle=a|v\rangle\), then \(A(B|v\rangle)=B(A|v\rangle)=aB|v\rangle\). So \(B|v\rangle\) remains in the same eigenspace of \(A\).

If an eigenspace of \(A\) is degenerate, we can choose a basis inside that eigenspace that also diagonalizes \(B\). Degeneracy is useful here, but it is not required for simultaneous diagonalization of commuting Hermitian operators.

Connection to angular momentum

For angular momentum, \(J^2\) commutes with \(J_z\): \([J^2,J_z]=0\). We can therefore choose common eigenstates \(|j,m\rangle\), with \(J^2|j,m\rangle=\hbar^2j(j+1)|j,m\rangle\) and \(J_z|j,m\rangle=\hbar m|j,m\rangle\).

For a given angular-momentum representation labelled by \(j\), the allowed \(m\) values run from \(-j\) to \(j\) in unit steps, giving \(2j+1\) states in the multiplet. This count follows from angular-momentum representation theory, not from \(J^2\) alone in every arbitrary physical system.
